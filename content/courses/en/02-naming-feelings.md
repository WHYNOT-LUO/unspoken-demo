## Why name your feelings?

Often we only say "annoyed", "upset" or "I'm fine". But "annoyed" might really be hurt, disappointment, or fear.

When we can tell which emotion we are feeling and find a more precise word for it, the emotion turns from a tangled mess into something we can see clearly and deal with. It also helps other people understand us.

## Some useful feeling words

- **Sad**: low, hurt, lonely, disappointed, heartbroken
- **Angry**: irritated, frustrated, offended, furious
- **Afraid**: worried, nervous, uneasy, scared
- **Happy**: relaxed, satisfied, grateful, understood
- **Mixed feelings**: guilty, ashamed, embarrassed, torn

You can have several feelings about the same thing. When your parents ask about your grades, you might feel **nervous**, **hurt** and a bit **angry**, all at once. That is completely normal.

## Notice your body's signals

Emotions often show up in the body first:

- a tight chest or throat
- a racing heart or sweaty palms
- stiff shoulders or an upset stomach

When you notice these signals, pause and ask yourself: "What am I feeling right now?"

## A simple sentence pattern

> I feel ______, because ______, and I hope ______.

For example: "I feel hurt, because I've been trying really hard, and I hope you'll listen to what I think first."

This pattern talks about **your own feelings and needs** rather than blaming the other person, so it is easier for them to hear.

## When an emotion is very strong

- Pause, take a few deep breaths, and slowly count to ten.
- Step away from whatever is upsetting you for a little while.
- Write down what you want to say first, then decide later, once you're calmer, whether and how to say it.

## Reflection

- Think of something small that happened today, and try writing one sentence with "I feel... because... and I hope...".
- Where in your body do your emotions usually show up first?
