## Why children don't talk

Many children do have things to say. They are worried that if they speak up, they'll be criticised, questioned, have it passed on to others, or disappoint their parents.

If, in the past, sharing something personal always led to a lecture, children slowly learn: "It's safer not to say anything."

## Listen first, then talk

Listening helps more than lecturing. When children feel heard, they are willing to keep talking.

- **Put your phone down and look at your child**: let them feel you are really listening.
- **Don't rush to give advice**: you can say "And then?" or "How did you feel then?"
- **Repeat back what you heard**: "It sounds like you felt really hurt that day." This helps your child feel understood.
- **Thank your child for telling you**: "Thank you for sharing this with me."

## When the topic makes you anxious

Some topics make parents especially anxious, for example when a child says they like someone, or that they're in a relationship.

What matters most then is to **steady your own emotions first**. If your first reaction is to scold or forbid, your child may well not tell you next time, and you lose the chance to understand and protect them.

You could try saying:

> "Thank you for telling me. I'd like to understand a bit more. What are things usually like between the two of you?"

Then, gradually, talk with your child about boundaries in relationships, how to protect themselves, and that they can always come to you if something makes them uncomfortable.

## Give your child a "safe channel"

Make it clear to your child: whatever happens, especially if they are in danger or get hurt, they can come to you, and you will help them first instead of blaming them first.

> "Whatever happens, you can tell me. We'll figure it out together."

## Reflection

- When did your child last choose to share something personal with you? How did you respond?
- If your child told you something that worried you a lot, what would you want your first words to be?
